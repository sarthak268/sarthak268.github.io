*Tracing the evolution of the brain, from simple neurons to human cognition, and what it means for AI 🧠*

### Convergent evolution

Evolution independently converges on common solutions. Wings evolved independently in insects, bats, and birds—their common ancestor did not have wings. The same is true of eyes.

### Photosynthesis, oxygen, and respiration

A single species of bacteria—cyanobacteria, or blue-green algae—found a profitable mechanism for extracting and storing energy: photosynthesis. This led to vast regions of the ocean being covered by them.

An event happened 2.4 billion years ago: the Great Oxidation Event, when oxygen levels skyrocketed. Being extremely reactive, oxygen compounds interfere with cellular processes, including the maintenance of DNA. Hence, antioxidants—compounds that remove oxygen from the bloodstream—are considered to prevent cancer. Photosynthetic lifeforms became victims of their success, causing one of the deadliest extinction periods in Earth's history.

A new form of bacteria then emerged that produced energy not through photosynthesis, but through cellular respiration: oxygen and sugar are converted into energy and carbon dioxide.

Life on Earth fell into perhaps the greatest symbiosis ever found between two competing and complementary forms of life: one category was photosynthetic, and the other was respiratory.

### Fungi and animals

Multicellular organisms have two ways to feed. Fungi chose the strategy of waiting, while animals chose the strategy of killing. The fungal strategy was more successful: by biomass, there is six times more fungus than animals. But throughout history, we have seen that the worse strategy is where more innovation emerges.

### How neurons encode the world

The range of natural variables is far greater than the firing rate of a neuron. This means that a neuron needs to encode a range of natural variables that can vary by over a factor of a million, all within a firing-rate range of only 0 to 500 spikes per second.

Neurons have a smart solution: there is no fixed relationship between the two. Neurons are always adapting their firing rates to their environment; they are constantly remapping the relationship between variables in the natural world and the language of firing rate.

### Dopamine and serotonin

In a nematode, dopamine is released when food is detected around the worm, whereas serotonin is released when food is detected inside the worm.

What happens when you see something you want—food when hungry, a sexy mate, or the finish line at the end of a race? The brain releases dopamine. What happens when you get something you want—when you are orgasming, eating delicious food, or simply finishing a task on your to-do list? The brain releases serotonin.

Dopamine is not a signal for pleasure itself; it is a signal for the anticipation of future pleasure.
